Hidden Driveway

by Werner A. Low

AJ had the Hidden Driveway idea as we were driving home from the courthouse, where my (perjured) testimony had enabled him to beat a traffic ticket that I would have had to pay for him.  Not to mention the higher insurance premiums that would have ensued.  He knew I was mad and that I was about to tell him, while I had him trapped in the car, that this was the last straw, the third and final ultimatum.  Get a job or get out.  Buster.  I don’t think he had the Hidden Driveway idea specifically to throw me off this track but the timing worked out that way.  Just as I was unclenching my jaw to speak his eyes popped and he cried out, like Archimedes leaping from the tub, “That’s it!  Yes, that’s definitely it!”

I should explain that instead of getting a job flipping burgers or dipping donuts, like his stoner pals, my son’s plan was to emulate Bill Gates, who’d also dropped out of college, and create a multi-billion dollar business in what had once been our garage.  One of AJ’s previous multi-billion dollar ideas was sub-audible music that would help plants grow faster, eliminating global hunger, as well as spot shortages of a certain broad leafed plant.  Another was the VCJ, or Voice Controlled Joystick.  Since neither of those ideas had lived up to its full potential it was with some skepticism that I asked, “It?  What’s the ‘it,’ AJ?’”

“Hidden driveways,” he whispered, softly, so no competitors could hear.  “There was a sign back there that said ‘Hidden Driveway,’ and that’s what gave me the idea.”

“What idea?” I asked, keeping my eyes on the road and both hands on the wheel, in the ten o’clock and two o’clock positions.

“I could install them,” he explained, as if this should have been obvious from his first statement.

“And, um, what would that involve, specifically?”

“I’d make it so you couldn’t see them.  You know, instead of having an ugly piece of blacktop out front, it would all be nice green lawn.  Or whatever.”

In contrast to AJ’s other ideas, which had all had glimmers of plausibility, this one seemed so completely ridiculous that I thought he might be mocking my linear thought process, but when I snuck a look at him he didn’t have a sarcastic air.  To the contrary, his eyes were wide with childlike wonder.

“Well,” I said, and then cleared my throat.  “Even supposing that something like that was possible, I can’t imagine that it would be feasible commercially.  I mean, can you think of any other example of a thing like that on the market?”

Without a moment’s hesitation, and without any malice whatsoever, he replied, “Sure, the invisible fence.”

I hate it when he does that.

#

I decided, in bad faith I’ll admit, to use AJ’s Hidden Driveway idea to my advantage.  That is, I agreed to provide a relatively small amount of seed money, but with the caveat that he had three months to show clear progress and definite feasibility.  If not, then it would be “game over,” and either he’d take a full-time job and start paying rent, or – this being his third strike – he’d be out.  We shook hands on it and I felt a pang of guilt when he smiled and thanked me for having faith in him.

I don’t know what kind of hours Bill Gates and crew worked in the early days but I’m pretty sure AJ worked less.  He’d be asleep when I left in the morning and playing video games in his room when I returned at 6:30, his pupils fully dilated.  He claimed this was all part of his creative method and I didn’t complain because each wasted day brought us closer to the deadline.

Driving home from the city on September 15th, I imagined the scene that would soon take place.  AJ would be up in his room blasting aliens with the prototype Voice Controlled Joystick.  Speaking softly, to not upset the program, which reacted unpredictably to abstract words, he’d say that he was really, really close and ask for a one month extension and some more money.  And I would say, calmly and unemotionally, that I couldn’t do that.  He could continue working on the HDP in the evening after he got home from Fabu-Burger, and fund it with the $7.50 an hour he’d be earning, but a deal was a deal and someday, when he was president of the Fabu-Burger Diversified Empire, he’d thank me for my firmness.  Either that or I might suggest that a year or two in the military might do as much for him as it had for me.

Confident of my message, I turned onto our street.  But when I went to pull into the driveway …

That’s right, it wasn't there.

Instead, AJ was standing where the driveway used to be with a huge, shit-eating grin.

“Just pull in,” he said, like a maitre d’ showing me to a wonderful table.

I did so, using the garage door as a target.  And it was weird, because while it looked like I was driving onto a blurry lawn I heard the crunch of gravel under the wheels.  It was even weirder when I got out of the car.  I could hear, and even feel, the gray stones under my feet, but I couldn’t quite see them.

#

To make a long story short, I provided second-tier financing.  To iron out some technical bugs.  For a patent attorney.  And marketing.

I thought the fellow Matt hired to take the photos for the brochure was too expensive, but I guess he was worth it.  I mean you couldn’t see the driveway at all.

Matt said his target market was anyone with a sign that said, “Private,” “Keep Out,” or “Beware of Dog.”  Republicans, for the most part, he said, then looked sheepish and said he didn’t mean any disrespect.  Which he truly didn’t.

That night a bunch of his stoner friends came over to celebrate and a couple of them parked their clunkers on the lawn.  They’d done that many times in the past, but now they had the perfect excuse.

#

I wasn't surprised when problems arose.  For example, it looked odd how only the driveway had no weeds or crab grass.  On high humidity days its greenness quivered.  And when it rained, mist hovered over the non-grass like a mirage.  I said that I thought snow would be even more difficult to handle, but AJ talked about a circuit that would turn the device off under specific conditions.  Of course all these tweaks cost money and AJ still hadn’t found a paying customer, even at break-even pricing.

A sure sign that one of AJ’s ideas has lost traction is when, before quite finishing Project A, he diversifies into variations A1, A2a or even B1, so when he said he was thinking of other applications for the hidden driveway technology I was skeptical.

“Such as?”

“What about hiding bald spots?”

I laughed and said I wasn't sure I’d want grass on my head.

He laughed, innocently.  “With hair, I mean.”  Then said, “Or what about hidden cameras that are really, really hidden.”

“I could see that,” I admitted.

“Or not,” he grinned.

I don’t know who he else he mentioned these ideas to but it wasn't long before two men wearing identical suits and sunglasses came to the house and asked for AJ.

“He isn't here,” I said.

“They looked at each other, smiled knowingly, and then sat on the couch, lowering themselves carefully, as if they were worried about sitting on a pair of reading glasses.  I offered them diet sodas but they declined.  A polite moment later the tall one turned to the shorter one and asked him if he’d had a chance to look at my tax returns yet.  The shorter one said yes, he had.  He raised an eyebrow and looked at me as he told the taller one that he thought it was a bit “two faced” of a man to lecture his son on responsibility and civil obedience when he was “fudging” his own taxes.  But when AJ came in they were suddenly as friendly as basketball recruiters. 

AJ and the two men went out to the garage.  They were there for hours.  After they left, AJ said that I didn’t need to worry about being audited.  Also, he thought that he would be able to pay me back soon.  Then, looking sad, I thought, he said he was sorry but he had to go away for a while.

#

At first I was happy to have the house to myself and be able to keep things just the way I wanted them.  But after a month I began to feel lonely.  It helped when, a couple days later, a direct deposit equal to exactly the total amount that AJ owed me was direct deposited to my account.  A month after that, exactly ten times the money I’d invested in the Hidden Driveway Project showed up in my account.  And a month after that – also on the first of the month – an even larger amount appeared.

It’s now been three months since I last saw AJ, but I think he’s been here a few times just recently.  Once the front door was left unlocked like he always did, even though I must have told him a hundred times that using the key flips the lock and you have to flip it back.  Another time there was food all over the kitchen counter, and just the kind of stuff he used to leave there – ham and cheese, bread, even the mayo.  It also looked like some video games were missing from his room.  And once I went out to the garage to get a rake and I could swear that I smelled dope.

Each time this happened I had the same emotional reaction.  Instead of feeling mad, I felt nostalgic.  I wished that I hadn't yelled at him so much about the front door.  Or gone on and on about how spoiled mayonnaise can give you salmonella, especially since it isn't true anymore, now that it’s no longer made from raw eggs.  I wished I hadn't added every little thing to the list of stuff he owed me for, or done the exact calculation about how much he would have earned if he’d gotten paid a minimum wage for all the hours he spent playing video games.  I even wished that just one night I’d said, “Hey, how about I try one of those funny cigarettes with you.”

For all his faults, I missed him.  And I wondered if, wherever he was, he felt the same way.

#

It snowed last night – just a few inches – and as I was making my morning coffee I suddenly had the idea to look and see how the hidden driveway had handled the white stuff.  It looked all white, but I couldn’t see it that well from the kitchen, so I went to the front door.  Even before I touched the handle I could tell that the door was unlocked.  I opened it slowly, like it might explode.  And then there was something else.  Evidently the thousand dollar cut-off sensor had worked because the driveway was covered with natural looking snow.  Not only that, but there were tire tracks where someone had driven in and parked next to me.  There didn’t appear to be a car at the end of the tracks but there were footsteps leading from that spot right to the front door where I was standing, and they looked like AJ’s size.

I closed the door quietly and walked into the dining room, which we never used, even when Martha was here.  AJ’s bedroom was directly above that room and, standing there, with one hand resting lightly on the mahogany of the table, like it was a Ouija board, I tried to sense whether my son was up in his room or not.

I had the distinct feeling that he was.  And I still do.  And the later it gets the surer I am that he’s there, because the little shit never gets up before noon.
